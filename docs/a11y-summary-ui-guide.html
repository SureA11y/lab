How to Read the A11y Summary UI

This document explains what each UI block, label, and number means, in plain language. It is written for non-technical readers who want to understand what the report is saying, what data it is based on, and how to interpret the ranges and status labels consistently across pages.

What this UI is

Think of this report as a triage dashboard.

It helps you decide which pages deserve manual accessibility testing first, and it gives you clues about why. It combines automated testing signals and structural signals. It does not claim WCAG conformance.

What it is designed to answer

  • Does this page look risky, based on what tools can detect?
  • How much can we trust the automated signals on this page?
  • Is the page structurally simple or complex from an accessibility point of view?
  • Which areas deserve manual checks, for example custom controls or hidden content?

What it is not

  • It is not a WCAG pass or fail result.
  • It is not a user experience evaluation with assistive technologies.
  • It is not a substitute for keyboard, screen reader, and design review.

UI layout

Reading top to bottom, a scanned page's report is organized into five parts, in this order.

1) Scan counts

Tool outcomes, at a glance

A compact count line (fail / can't tell / pass) appears first, before anything else.

2) Banner + narrative

Decision hint, and why

A short status banner (five possible tones, not only "review needed"), followed by an auto-generated paragraph explaining the reasoning and what to validate.

3) Screenshots + issues table

The findings themselves

Captured screenshots (if any), then the detailed table of individual findings with their fail/review/pass status.

4) Headline score cards

Summary scores

The score cards (three on a single page, four on a portfolio) - last on the page, not first.

5) Supporting metrics

Why the scores look that way

Detailed signals about structure and controls, shown as numbers and sometimes mapped to intervals.

Banner

The banner has five tones - it's not always a warning.

Two of its five possible titles are genuinely positive. Reading the banner as inherently cautionary is a common misunderstanding - a page can render a "No automated findings" banner even while carrying a minor complexity signal like an iframe, if the score and confidence are otherwise high.

The five titles

SeverityTitle shownGeneral meaning
bad“Issues detected”At least one automated violation was found. Always the highest-attention tone.
warn“Review recommended”The scan was capped, has incomplete checks, low confidence, or another flag requiring manual review - and there are no outright violations.
ok“Quick review advised”No strong attention cue, but the overall score band isn't clearly good.
good“No automated findings”No violations, no manual-review flags, and the score band is good.
unknown“More review needed”Fallback when the score band itself can't be determined.

Where it comes from

Severity follows a priority order: any violation forces bad, regardless of everything else. Otherwise, a capped scan, incomplete checks, low confidence, or the "manual review needed" flag forces warn. Otherwise, the tone falls back to whichever band the overall adjusted score lands in (good/ok/warn/bad) - except a lone "warn" band with no other attention cue present is treated as ok rather than warn, so the banner doesn't cry wolf on a borderline score alone.

The specific reasons that can contribute to a warn/bad tone: incomplete automated checks, a capped scan, Shadow DOM present, iframes present, low automation confidence, high hidden/conditional content, many stateful components, and links or buttons that appear to lack an accessible name (either directly, or an icon-heavy heuristic when that metric has no defined threshold).

A sixth state: no banner at all.

When a page has zero violations, the "manual review needed" flag is false, and none of the attention cues above fired either, no banner is rendered - not even the good tone. The narrative paragraph is shown on its own. This is different from a good banner: good still renders when one of those cues fired (say, an iframe was present) but the score band and everything else stayed clean; the absent case is when there was nothing at all worth naming.

The narrative below the banner

The banner is immediately followed by an auto-generated paragraph with up to four headed sub-sections: Why this looks that way, Validate first, What to validate, and What to do next. Its exact wording rotates across a small set of variants per page (so re-scanning the same page doesn't always show byte-identical phrasing), but the underlying reasoning is driven by the same signals as the banner itself.

How to read it

  • A bad or warn banner: plan a short manual verification, at least for key user journeys.
  • A good banner does not mean the page is accessible - it means fewer automated and structural risk signals were detected. Manual validation still matters.

Headline score cards

These cards are the fastest way to understand the report. A single-page report shows three: Automated check result, Assessment Confidence, and Automated evidence. A portfolio/global report adds a fourth, Accessibility Risk Ind., alongside the same three - Assessment Confidence is not exclusive to single-page reports, it shows on both.

There is no separate "Structural signals" card.

DOM structure and complexity (semantic markup, hidden content, stateful components, and the rest) show up as individual rows in a collapsed "Page structure signals" section instead of a composite score - see Page structure signals below. They no longer feed the live confidence value either; that role is now filled by what the engine itself reports (see Assessment Confidence below).

Sometimes: no cards at all.

When too little of the page could actually be evaluated (too few testable elements to compute a density), the cards are replaced by a short statement explaining why, plus whatever violations/incomplete checks were still found - which are real findings even without a score to attach them to.

Automated check result

The one card that's always present, when any cards are shown. On a single page it's identical to the page's own adjusted score. On a portfolio summary, it's the mean of every scanned page's score.

What a low or high value usually suggests

  • Low: on average, pages found issues, or automation was less reliable, or both.
  • High: on average, pages looked structurally straightforward with few detected issues.

Plain reading

“If we have to pick one number for a dashboard, use this one, and still validate manually.”

Accessibility Risk Ind. Portfolio reports only

Shown alongside the other three cards when the report covers multiple pages. The conservative, combined dashboard signal: evidence discounted by the weakest per-page confidence across the whole portfolio - one badly-understood page pulls this number down for everyone.

What a low or high value usually suggests

  • Low: automation found issues somewhere, or at least one page is less reliable to assess, or both.
  • High: automation found few issues and every page looks structurally straightforward.

Automated evidence

A score derived from automated rule outcomes and how dense they are relative to how much of the page was testable. It reflects what the tool could detect, not everything that matters for users.

Typical interpretations

  • High: few detected issues, fewer severe findings, and fewer widespread matches.
  • Medium: some issues or uncertainty, or a mix of mild and moderate signals.
  • Low: more failures, higher severity, or widespread impact.

Do not overread this

A high automated score can still hide keyboard or screen reader problems that automation cannot detect reliably.

Assessment Confidence

A reliability indicator for the automated evidence, on both report types. It comes from the engine itself - the mean of the confidence surea11y reported on its findings - not from page structure, so it can be absent ("not measured") rather than guessed when the findings carry none. It is not a quality score, it is about trust in what automation could evaluate.

Typical interpretations

  • High: surea11y was confident in what it reported.
  • Medium: some uncertainty in what was reported, treat automation as a hint.
  • Low, or absent: surea11y was unsure, or its findings carried no confidence at all - treat automation as a rough hint only, manual review is likely required.

Quick mental model

Confidence answers “Should I trust the automated score here?”

Automated rule results

A compact count line appears at the very top of each scan's results, before the banner: e.g. 3 fail, 1 can't tell, 42 pass (each with its own glyph). Further down, the detailed issues table repeats the same three outcomes per individual finding, in its Status column.

Label What it means Practical implication
fail (table: “Fail”) The tool detected a likely accessibility failure. Treat as an item to investigate and fix, then retest.
can't tell (table: “Review”) The tool could not decide pass or fail reliably. Schedule manual verification, this is a frequent trigger for the banner.
pass (table: “Pass”) The rule passed for the scanned content. Useful, but not a guarantee, since automation covers only a subset of requirements.

The count line and the table use different label casing for the same three outcomes (lowercase "fail"/"can't tell"/"pass" in the count line, capitalized "Fail"/"Review"/"Pass" in the table) - they refer to the same underlying result, not two different classification systems.

Findings that need review: which way they lean, and how to check them

A "can't tell" finding is a question for a person. surea11y often says more about it. Where the page's evidence points one way, the question leans: a heading that skips a level likely fails, an aria-checked on a native checkbox likely passes. A lean never changes the outcome: the finding stays "can't tell" until a person confirms it.

  • In the table, a "Review" row that likely fails or likely passes carries a badge beside its status, and its count says how many of its questions lean each way (2 likely fail, 1 needs a person). Rows that likely fail come first, then those that likely pass, then those that need a person; by severity within each.
  • The count line says how many questions likely fail beside "can't tell" (4 can't tell (2 likely fail)), and the narrative's reason for review names them as the place to start.
  • In a row's details, each question shows its lean: why the evidence points that way, a caution when something could make it wrong, and, once surea11y has counted it against the W3C ACT test cases, how often a lean of its kind was right (right in 9 of 10 known cases).
  • Every question also says how to check it: a few steps, starting from the element it names, then what you see if it passes and if it fails, in the scan's language.

Tip for non-technical readers

A small number of failures can still be important if they block basic tasks. A large number of passes does not prove accessibility, it only proves that many automated checks did not detect a problem.

The narrative paragraph

Immediately below the banner, an auto-generated paragraph explains the reasoning behind it, in up to four headed parts (only the parts relevant to this page appear):

  • Why this looks that way - the specific signals driving the banner's tone.
  • Validate first - the single highest-priority thing to check manually.
  • What to validate - a broader list of things worth checking.
  • What to do next - concrete next actions.

Wording rotates across a small set of variants per page, so re-scanning the same page doesn't always produce byte-identical phrasing - the underlying reasoning stays tied to the same signals as the banner.

Page structure signals

These metrics describe the page structure. They aren't rolled into a composite score - they show up as individual rows under "Page structure signals" in the report, collapsed by default - but they help explain why some pages are harder to assess, even when automation looks clean, and they're what scores.evidenceByDom/confidenceByDom (the superseded model, kept for comparison only) are built from.

Metric label What it reflects Examples of what different values can mean
Semantic markup How much the page uses meaningful HTML structures (landmarks, headings, lists, form elements, tables), rather than only generic containers.
  • Higher: content is likely easier to navigate and reason about.
  • Lower: heavy use of generic containers, often requires more ARIA and more careful testing.
Content coverage A proxy for how much of the DOM contains elements that are worth evaluating from an accessibility point of view. This can drop when a page has many decorative or repeated layout nodes.
  • High: the DOM is mostly meaningful content and controls.
  • Low: large layout scaffolding, heavy frameworks, or complex rendering patterns.
Hidden content The share of elements that appear hidden (for example via hidden attributes, aria-hidden, or computed styles).
  • Low: little conditional content, fewer risks around visibility and announcements.
  • High: accordions, tabs, modals, virtualized lists, and conditional panels are more likely.
Max DOM depth How deeply nested the page structure is. Deep structures tend to be harder to navigate and harder to audit.
  • Shallow: simpler layout, usually easier reading order and focus order.
  • Deep: complex layout, more chances of odd focus sequences and reading order problems.
Stateful components A count of patterns that imply UI state changes, for example dialogs, details elements, tablists, and expanded states.
  • Low: mostly static content pages.
  • High: application like UI, more keyboard and screen reader behavior to validate.
Focusable density (per 1k) Number of focusable elements per 1,000 DOM nodes - how interaction-dense the page is overall.
  • Low: mostly reading content, fewer focus stops.
  • High: many interactive elements, more focus order and keyboard-navigation surface to validate.
Iframes Whether the page embeds content in iframes. Some embedded content may not be analyzable by the same scan, depending on context.
  • 0: everything is in the main document, simpler assessment scope.
  • 1 or more: check whether iframe content is in scope and scanned, manual checks may be needed.
Shadow hosts Whether Web Components with Shadow DOM are present. Depending on tooling and configuration, Shadow DOM can reduce automation reach.
  • 0: typical DOM, automation reach is more predictable.
  • 1 or more: treat confidence as lower, test custom components manually.
Capped metrics Whether the scan stopped early because the page was too large or too complex to traverse fully within limits.
  • No: metrics reflect the full sampled DOM.
  • Yes: metrics are partial, interpret results with caution, manual validation is recommended.

Interactive controls

This section focuses on links and buttons because they represent the primary interaction layer. It highlights density and labeling signals that often correlate with usability problems.

Metric label What it reflects Examples of what different values can mean
Links per 1k How link heavy the UI is, normalized by page size. This is a complexity signal, not a defect signal.
  • Low: content pages, fewer navigation targets.
  • High: menus, tables, dense navigation, more focus targets to validate.
Link text share How much of the visible text belongs to links. Extreme values can indicate either link heavy navigation or link sparse content.
  • Very low: many links may be icons or controls without visible text, check labeling.
  • Very high: content is mostly links, check clarity and focus order.
Links with no visible text The fraction of visible links that do not show text. These may be icon links and require accessible names.
  • Near 0: most links are self describing visually.
  • Higher: many icon links, verify accessible names and touch targets.
Links no text and no name A stronger risk signal: links without visible text and without a basic naming hint (for example aria-label, aria-labelledby, title).
  • 0: good sign, icon links are likely labeled.
  • Above 0: investigate quickly, these are often unusable for screen reader users.
Buttons per 1k How button heavy the UI is, normalized by page size. High density suggests application like complexity.
  • Low: content pages, fewer actions.
  • High: complex interaction flows, validate keyboard behavior and names.
Button text share Share of the page's total visible text that comes from buttons. The button equivalent of "Link text share" above.
  • Very low: many buttons may be icon-only, check labeling.
  • Very high: an action-heavy page, check clarity and focus order.
Buttons with no visible text The fraction of buttons that do not show text. These are often icon buttons and require accessible names and states.
  • Near 0: most buttons are explicit actions with text.
  • Higher: icon heavy UI, verify names, pressed states, expanded states, and focus.
Buttons no text and no name A strong risk signal: buttons without visible text and without a basic naming hint.
  • 0: good sign.
  • Above 0: investigate quickly, often blocks assistive technology users.

Important note

“No name” here is based on cheap signals only. A control might still have a correct accessible name via other mechanisms. Treat these as prioritization hints, then confirm using browser accessibility inspection and assistive technology testing.

Ranges and intervals

Many values are shown on a 0 to 100 scale, or mapped into bands such as Low, Moderate, Good, High. These are called intervals. Intervals exist to make the UI readable and consistent across pages.

Why intervals exist

  • Consistency: a number becomes a stable label, so readers can compare pages quickly.
  • Triage: teams need a signal that suggests “investigate” versus “monitor”.
  • Noise reduction: small DOM changes should not change the interpretation drastically.
  • Communication: non-technical stakeholders can understand labels faster than raw metrics.

Why the interval boundaries are chosen the way they are

Intervals are pragmatic thresholds, not universal truths.

The boundaries are chosen to align with typical web page patterns and practical audit risk. They aim to separate “common and usually fine” from “less common and often risky”. They also consider what automation can and cannot see reliably.

Examples of how to interpret banding

Kind of metric How banding is typically used Example meanings
Score (0 to 100) A label such as Moderate or High is applied to reduce over-precision and guide action.
  • Moderate: results are mixed, plan some manual checks.
  • High: fewer risk signals, still validate key journeys.
Rates and shares (0 to 1) Bands identify when a proportion is high enough to be suspicious, for example many controls without visible text.
  • Near 0: likely low risk for that specific pattern.
  • Higher: prioritize manual checks on naming and discoverability.
Per 1k density metrics Bands are often used as a complexity indicator. Higher density means more focus targets and more states to validate.
  • Low density: often simpler content pages.
  • High density: application like UI, more risk of focus and name problems.

How to use intervals responsibly

  • Use them to decide where to look first, not to declare conformance.
  • When a value is near a boundary, treat it as “around that band”, not as a hard action.
  • If your product type is unusual, consider adjusting thresholds based on real data from your pages.

Interpretation examples

These examples explain how the same metric can mean different things, depending on context. They are illustrative and do not represent any specific scan.

Example: High automated score, low confidence

The tool finds few problems, but the page is complex (many custom controls, Shadow DOM, lots of hidden panels).

  • Interpretation: automation likely missed interaction issues.
  • Action: prioritize manual keyboard and screen reader checks on key workflows.

Example: Medium automated score, clean page structure

The page uses semantic HTML and native controls (clean "Page structure signals"), but automation still detects a few rule failures.

  • Interpretation: issues may be straightforward to fix and verify.
  • Action: fix violations, rerun scans, then validate one key journey manually.

Example: Many icon controls, naming risk metrics rise

Buttons and links without visible text increase, and some also show no basic naming hints.

  • Interpretation: high probability of missing accessible names.
  • Action: audit accessible names using accessibility tree inspection, then screen reader verification.

Example: Capped scan

The scan stops early due to size limits, so metrics reflect only part of the page.

  • Interpretation: treat all scores as partial.
  • Action: run targeted scans on key regions, and plan manual validation.

What to do next

For product owners

  • Use the indicator to prioritize which pages need manual validation first.
  • If the banner appears, schedule verification before release or reporting.
  • Track trends over time, focus on improving the indicator and reducing repeated risk patterns.

For developers

  • Fix automated violations first, rerun, then confirm manually.
  • Investigate controls without visible text, verify accessible names and states.
  • If Page structure signals show heavy complexity, or confidence is reduced, budget time for interaction testing.

For testers

  • Use the report to select representative pages and journeys.
  • When incomplete checks exist, validate the specific patterns they refer to.
  • Validate focus order, visible focus, state announcements, and labeling.

Limits and common misunderstandings

Do not use this report as a compliance statement.

These metrics are decision aids. They support prioritization and consistency, but they cannot confirm conformance with standards on their own.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Many passes means the page is accessible”: passes mean the automated rules did not detect issues, not that issues do not exist.
  • “High confidence means high accessibility”: confidence means the tool results are more representative, not that the page is good.
  • “Complex Page structure signals mean the page is inaccessible”: those signals are about complexity and audit friendliness, not user outcomes.
